Lecture 01 · Political Science as a Discipline
Political Science as a Science: Development & Place in Social Disciplines
Methods Foundations Interdisciplinary
📋
Lecture Summary
›

Political science is the systematic study of power, governance, and political life. It evolved from ancient Greek philosophy (Aristotle calling politics the "master science") through modern American behavioral science. The discipline uses both quantitative (surveys, statistics) and qualitative (case studies, historical analysis) methods.

Politics derives from the Greek polis (city-state) and encompasses all activities within and among states. It can be understood as: the art of government, state activity, resource allocation, or a social process involving power and conflict.

🔑 Core Insight: Harold Lasswell's definition — "politics is the study of who gets what, when and how" — remains the most concise and exam-tested definition. David Easton's "authoritative allocation of values" is equally important.
🔤
Key Concepts & Definitions
8 terms
›
Polis
Ancient Greek city-state; root of the word "politics". ~700 existed in Greece.
Political Science
Systematic, empirical and normative study of power, institutions, and decision-making.
Quantitative Methods
Statistical, numerical analysis — surveys, electoral data, mathematical models.
Qualitative Methods
Case studies, historical analysis, comparative analysis — non-numerical insights.
Sovereignty
Supreme, absolute authority of the state within its territory (Jean Baudin).
Separation of Powers
Division of government into legislative, executive, judicial branches (Montesquieu).
Comparative Politics
Subfield comparing political systems across countries (e.g., UK vs USA).
Political Economy
Examines how resource distribution and economic inequality shape governance.

Lecture 02 · History of Political Thought
Development of Political Thought in the History of Civilizations
Ancient East Greek Philosophy Renaissance
📋
Lecture Summary
›

Political thought evolved from divine-right monarchies in the Ancient East (Mesopotamia, Egypt, China) through Greek rationalism (Plato, Aristotle), Roman pragmatism, Medieval theological politics, Renaissance realism (Machiavelli), and Enlightenment social contract theory (Locke, Rousseau).

Central trajectory: from gods legitimize rulers → reason legitimizes rulers → the people legitimize rulers (social contract).

🔑 Key Thread: The "Mandate of Heaven" (China) to the "Social Contract" (Rousseau) represents the evolution of political legitimacy — from divine to popular sovereignty.
🧠
Key Thinkers by Era
8 thinkers
›
Plato
428–348 BCE · Athens
Ideal state ruled by philosopher-kings. In The Republic, justice means each class doing its proper role. Favored epistocracy (rule by the wise).
Idealism
Aristotle
384–322 BCE · Athens
Empirical approach — studied 158 constitutions. "Man is a political animal." Preferred polity (constitutional government). Classified 6 regime types.
Empiricism
Niccolò Machiavelli
1469–1527 · Florence
In The Prince: power must be understood on its own terms. "Better to be feared than loved." Separated politics from morality. Founded political realism.
Political Realism
Jean Baudin
1530–1596 · France
First to use "political science." Defined sovereignty as "absolute and perpetual power of the state." Pioneer of state-centered theory.
Sovereignty
Jean-Jacques Rousseau
1712–1778 · Geneva
The Social Contract: legitimate authority from freely agreed social contract. Key concept: the "general will" represents collective interests. Inspired the French Revolution.
Democracy
Edmund Burke
1729–1797 · Ireland
Father of conservatism. Against radical change; society is an organic, evolving organism. Tradition and custom over abstract theory. Criticized French Revolution.
Conservatism
Al-Farabi
872–950 · Kazakh/Islamic
"The Second Teacher" (after Aristotle). Adapted Aristotelian philosophy to Islamic context. "Virtuous City" requires philosopher-king with moral virtue. Emphasis on justice and governance ethics.
Islamic Political Thought
Thomas More
1478–1535 · England
In Utopia: ideal island society without private property, with religious tolerance and communal harmony guided by reason.
Utopianism

Exam Cheat Sheet
›

Evolution of Political Thought

  • Ancient East: Divine legitimacy; Mandate of Heaven; legal codes
  • Ancient Greece: Plato (Republic/Philosopher-King) → Aristotle (empirical/polity)
  • Renaissance: Machiavelli (realism, The Prince) → More (Utopia)
  • Early Modern: Baudin (sovereignty) → Montesquieu (separation of powers)
  • Enlightenment: Locke (natural rights) → Rousseau (general will) → Burke (conservatism)
  • Kazakh/Islamic: Al-Farabi (virtue) → Yasawi (spirituality) → Balasaguni (statecraft)

Two Stages of Modern Political Thought

  • Stage 1 (16th–18th c.): Civic conception — individual at center; state's right to coerce
  • Stage 2 (19th c.+): Social conception — groups (nation, class, strata) at center

Lecture 03 · Ethics, Morality & Political Traditions
Moral-Ethical Foundations & Major Political Traditions
Utilitarianism Marxism Social Contract
📋
Lecture Summary
›

This lecture explores three major political-ethical traditions: Utilitarianism (greatest happiness for greatest number), Marxism (class struggle and revolution), and Social Contract Theory (voluntary agreement between individuals and state). Additionally, two landmark experiments — the Stanford Prison Experiment and Milgram's Obedience Study — reveal how power structures and authority affect political behavior.

🔑 Central Question: What makes people obey and enforce unjust systems — and why do they stop questioning? Both Zimbardo and Milgram answer: structure and authority matter more than individual virtue.
🔤
Key Concepts
9 terms
›
Utilitarianism
Greatest happiness for the greatest number. Bentham: "the principle of utility." Mill: refined version acknowledging quality of pleasure.
Marxism
Society driven by class struggle. Ruling class controls means of production; exploits working class (proletariat).
Social Contract
Hypothetical agreement: individuals give up some freedoms to the state in exchange for protection and order.
Bourgeoisie
Capitalist ruling class that owns means of production (Marx).
Proletariat
Working class; sells labor to survive; exploited by bourgeoisie (Marx).
Communist Manifesto
1848 work by Marx & Engels; class struggle leads to proletarian revolution and classless society.
Trolley Problem
Utilitarian thought experiment: sacrifice one to save many — tests utility principle.
Obedience to Authority
Milgram: individuals comply with authority figures even against moral judgment.
Power Dynamics
Zimbardo: institutional roles and unchecked power can corrupt even ordinary individuals.
🧠
Key Thinkers
›
Jeremy Bentham
1748–1832 · England
Founded utilitarianism. "The greatest happiness for the greatest number." Created the Panopticon prison design — all actions should maximize collective utility.
Utilitarianism
John Stuart Mill
1806–1873 · England
Refined utilitarianism: quality matters, not just quantity of pleasure. "Better Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." On Liberty — harm principle.
Utilitarianism
Karl Marx
1818–1883 · Prussia/Germany
History = class struggle. Capitalism exploits the proletariat. Communist Manifesto (1848). Predicted inevitable proletarian revolution and classless society.
Marxism
Philip Zimbardo
1933–present · USA
Stanford Prison Experiment (1971): ordinary people in positions of power became abusive. Proved structure > individual virtue. Terminated after 6 days.
Psychology & Power
Stanley Milgram
1933–1984 · USA
Obedience Experiment: majority of participants administered electric shocks when ordered by authority. Showed individuals obey authority even against moral values.
Psychology & Authority

Exam Cheat Sheet
›

Three Political Traditions

  • Utilitarianism: Bentham → greatest happiness; Mill → quality matters; Trolley Problem
  • Marxism: Class struggle → bourgeoisie exploits proletariat → revolution → classless society
  • Social Contract: Locke/Hobbes/Rousseau → individuals consent to state in exchange for protection

  • Stanford Prison (Zimbardo, 1971): 24 participants; guards abused prisoners; ended in 6 days; shows institutional power corrupts

  • Both experiments: structure and authority shape behavior more than individual character
  • Political implication: institutions must have checks, accountability, and human rights protection

Lecture 04 · Political Power
Political Power: Essence and Mechanisms of Realization
5 Power Forms 3 Legitimacy Types Separation of Powers
📋
Lecture Summary
›

Political power is the capacity to influence events, decisions, and the behavior of others within a political system. Power is relational — it exists within social relationships and is constantly contested. Political power is distinguished by its supremacy and publicity, and its link to legitimate authority.

🔑 Key Framework: French & Raven's 5 Power Forms (1960): Coercive, Reward, Legitimate, Referent (Reference), Expert. Weber's 3 Legitimacy Types: Traditional, Charismatic, Rational-Legal. These are the most exam-tested classifications.
🔤
Key Concepts
10 terms
›
Coercive Power
Power through threat of punishment or negative consequences. E.g., police arrest powers.
Reward Power
Power through offering incentives. E.g., scholarships, bonuses, promotions.
Legitimate Power
Derived from formal position or authority (teacher, elected official, parent).
Referent Power
Based on personal charisma, admiration, or identification. Celebrity influence.
Expert Power
Based on knowledge, skills, or expertise. Doctors, scientists, economists.
Traditional Legitimacy
Authority from customs and tradition (monarchies, tribal leaders).
Charismatic Legitimacy
Authority from exceptional personal qualities (revolutionary leaders, prophets).
Rational-Legal Legitimacy
Authority from rules, laws, and formal procedures (modern states, bureaucracies).
Checks & Balances
Mechanisms preventing any one branch from dominating; ensures institutional accountability.
Separation of Powers
Legislative, Executive, Judicial branches — Montesquieu's doctrine preventing tyranny.

Lecture 05 · Political Ideologies
Political Ideologies: From Classical to Contemporary
Liberalism Conservatism Socialism Feminism
📋
Lecture Summary
›

Political ideologies are coherent belief systems that describe society, prescribe change, and mobilize people. They exist on a left-right spectrum originating from the French Revolution (1789). Classical ideologies include Liberalism, Conservatism, Socialism, and Nationalism. Contemporary ideologies include Environmentalism, Feminism, and Multiculturalism.

🔑 Bobbio's Key Insight: The fundamental distinction between left and right is their attitude toward equality. The left sees equality as desirable and achievable; the right treats it as either impossible or undesirable.

  • Conservatism: tradition, hierarchy, organic society, anti-radical change (Burke)
  • Socialism: equality, collectivism, critique of capitalism (Marx/Engels → Social Democracy)

  • Feminism: challenge patriarchy; 3 waves; intersectionality (3rd wave)
  • Multiculturalism: respect for cultural diversity; critique of assimilation

Left vs Right (Bobbio)

  • LEFT: equality, progress, intervention, collectivism
  • RIGHT: hierarchy, tradition, order, individual responsibility

Lecture 06 · Political Culture & Socialization
Political Culture, Ideology & Socialization
Almond & Verba 3 Culture Types
📋
Lecture Summary
›

Political culture refers to the shared values, beliefs, and orientations of a society toward its political system. Gabriel Almond and Sidney Verba (The Civic Culture, 1963) identified three types: Parochial (low awareness), Subject (passive recipients), and Participant (active citizens). Political socialization is the lifelong process of acquiring political values through family, education, peers, and media.

🔑 Almond & Verba's 3 Culture Types: Parochial (traditional), Subject (authoritarian-compatible), Participant (democracy-compatible). Kazakhstan shows elements of participant culture in elections but retains subject culture in deference to central authority.
🔤
Key Concepts
›
Political Culture
Society's predominant beliefs, values, and attitudes toward its political system and citizens' roles within it.
Parochial Culture
Citizens have little awareness of political institutions. Politics viewed as remote; loyalty is local/familial.
Subject Culture
Citizens are aware of the system but see themselves as passive subjects; compatible with authoritarian regimes.
Participant Culture
Citizens actively engage, see themselves as shapers of policy; most compatible with democracy.
Political Socialization
Lifelong process by which individuals acquire political knowledge, values, and behavioral orientations.
Cognitive Orientation
Knowledge and beliefs about political institutions (Almond & Verba).
Affective Orientation
Emotional feelings toward political actors and systems (Almond & Verba).
Evaluative Orientation
Judgments and opinions about the political order (Almond & Verba).

Lecture 07 · Political Systems
Political Systems, Regimes & Democratic Theory
Democracy Authoritarianism Totalitarianism
📋
Lecture Summary
›

Political systems are the organized structures through which a society governs itself. Regimes differ by who holds power, how it is legitimized, and how it is exercised. The three major regime types are: Democracy (rule by the people, free elections, rights), Authoritarianism (concentrated power, limited pluralism), and Totalitarianism (total control of all spheres of life).

🔑 Key Distinction: Authoritarianism limits political participation; Totalitarianism controls all aspects of life — economic, social, cultural. Nazism (Germany) and Stalinism (USSR) are the classic examples of totalitarianism.

Lecture 08 · Electoral Systems & Political Parties
Electoral Systems, Political Parties & Party Systems
Duverger's Law Party Types Party Systems
📋
Lecture Summary
›

Political parties are organizations united around shared ideology to contest elections and govern. Duverge (France) classified parties into Kader (elite/caucus) and Mass parties. These evolved into Catch-All parties. Duverger's Law (1940s) states: single-member plurality elections → two-party systems; proportional representation → multi-party systems. Party systems range from no-party (authoritarian) to multi-party (most democracies).

🔑 Duverger's Law: Electoral systems shape party systems. Plurality (first-past-the-post) favors two-party systems (USA, UK). Proportional representation favors multi-party systems (most of Europe).

Lecture 09 · Political Modernization
Political Modernization, Westernization & Development Theory

📋
Lecture Summary
›

Political modernization is the transformation of a society's political system toward more participatory, institutionalized, and complex forms. Westernization refers to the adoption of Western values (democracy, capitalism, secularism). Modernization Theory distinguishes two paths: Primary (organic, bottom-up, Western Europe/USA) and Secondary (catch-up, top-down, imposed by elites: Russia, Turkey, Kazakhstan).

Lecture 10 · Global Policy & Balance of Power
Global Policy Actors & the Balance of Power in International Relations
IGOs NGOs Polarity Systems
📋
Lecture Summary
›

Global policy actors are institutions, states, organizations, and individuals that shape cross-border policy. They include nation-states, Intergovernmental Organizations (IGOs) like the UN, WHO, NATO, Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs), and Multinational Corporations (MNCs). The balance of power can be unipolar (USA post-Cold War), bipolar (Cold War: USA vs USSR), or multipolar (emerging today). Norm entrepreneurs (Greta Thunberg, Malala Yousafzai) can also shape global discourse.

🔑 Key Framework: Balance of Power has three configurations: Unipolar (one dominant power), Bipolar (two superpowers), Multipolar (several great powers). The post-Cold War era saw US unipolarity; today the world shifts toward multipolarity (China, Russia, EU, USA).
🔤
Key Global Actors
›
United Nations (1945)
193 member states; peace, human rights, development. Addresses COVID, climate change (UNFCCC).
WHO (1948)
UN's global health agency. Coordinated COVID-19 response, vaccine distribution, health standards.
NATO (1949)
Political-military alliance for collective defense. Originally vs USSR; now reaffirmed by Russia-Ukraine war.
UNESCO (1945)
Education, science, culture, communication. Protects World Heritage sites; promotes inclusive education.
MNCs
Multinational corporations (Apple, Shell, Samsung); shift economies with investment decisions.
NGOs
Non-governmental organizations (Amnesty International, Doctors Without Borders); human rights, humanitarian aid.
Norm Entrepreneurs
Individuals who reshape values and pressure states (Greta Thunberg, Malala Yousafzai). Constructivist theory.
Multi-vector Policy
Kazakhstan's foreign policy balancing Russia, China, EU, USA simultaneously.
