Lecture 01

Political Science as a Science: Development and Place in the System of Social Disciplines

From the Greek polis to modern academia — how politics became a science.

Comprehensive Summary

Political science is the systematic, empirical and normative study of power, governance, institutions and decision-making. The discipline traces its intellectual roots to ancient Greece — specifically to Aristotle's declaration that politics is the "master science" governing all human affairs. Over centuries, it evolved from a branch of philosophy into a rigorous social science employing both quantitative and qualitative methods. Today it encompasses comparative politics, international relations, political theory, public law, public policy and public administration. It is inherently interdisciplinary, drawing on sociology, economics, psychology, law, history and media studies to produce comprehensive analyses of political life.

The course focuses on Kazakhstan's place in global political discourse while exploring universal themes: the nature of power, the origins of the state, the mechanics of representation, the role of ideology, and — crucially — the emerging threats of the digital age to national security and democratic governance.

Core Concepts & Definitions

Politics

From the Greek polis (city-state). In the broadest sense, politics encompasses all human activities relating to governance, authority and collective decision-making. Harold Lasswell's classic definition: "the study of who gets what, when, and how."

Political Science

The academic discipline that systematically studies political phenomena — institutions, behavior, ideas and outcomes — using empirical and normative methods. Jean Bodin first used the term "political science" in the 16th century.

Politics as Arena

Political behavior is confined to formal institutions — parliaments, courts, executive offices. Politics happens within designated structures (Andrew Heywood's framework).

Politics as Process

Political behavior can occur in any social context involving power, conflict or consensus-seeking — in families, corporations, universities, NGOs (Colin Hay's broader view).

Authoritative Allocation of Values

David Easton's definition of politics: governments respond to societal demands and distribute benefits, obligations and sanctions. Shifts focus from institutions to outcomes.

Sovereignty

The supreme authority of the state over its territory and population. Jean Bodin defined it as "absolute and perpetual power." A cornerstone of modern international law.

Polis

The ancient Greek city-state — the foundational unit of political life. Roughly 700 poleis existed in ancient Greece, ranging from Athens (~40,000 citizens) to smaller communities.

Interdisciplinary Character

Political science integrates insights from history, economics, sociology, psychology, law, cultural studies and media — no isolated discipline can fully explain political phenomena.

Five Functions of Political Science

  1. Descriptive — identifying and organizing facts about political life (elections, institutions, decision processes).
  2. Explanatory — analyzing causes and dynamics (why do some democracies survive while others fail?).
  3. Predictive — providing probabilistic forecasts (election results, policy impacts, risks of instability).
  4. Normative — addressing questions of justice, legitimacy, equality and democracy.
  5. Practical — applying knowledge to governance, public policy and international relations.

Model Answer: Definition of Politics (Q1)

The word "politics" derives from the ancient Greek polis, meaning city-state — the primary unit of political organization in ancient Greece. For Aristotle, what occurred within the polis was inherently political, encompassing all collective decisions about governance, justice and the common good. This suggests that politics is not merely about elections or government, but about the totality of organized human life. As the discipline evolved, this scope expanded: while early modern theorists like Bodin confined politics to the state and its sovereignty, 20th-century scholars like Lasswell ("who gets what, when, how") and Easton ("authoritative allocation of values") shifted focus to outcomes and resource distribution. Contemporary scholars such as Heywood and Hay further broaden politics beyond the state to include any social context involving power, conflict or consensus-seeking.

Lecture 02

Development of Political Thought in the History of Civilizations

From the Mandate of Heaven to Enlightenment — tracing the lineage of political ideas.

Comprehensive Summary

Political thought did not emerge in a vacuum. It evolved from the earliest class-based societies of the ancient East — where rulers were divine intermediaries — through the rational philosophies of Greece and Rome, the theological frameworks of medieval Christendom, the secular pragmatism of the Renaissance, the social contract theories of the Enlightenment and the critical reformism of 19th-century Kazakhstan. Each era produced thinkers who responded to the political crises, social structures and intellectual currents of their time. The story of political thought is, fundamentally, the story of humanity's changing relationship with authority, justice and governance.

Model Answer: Plato vs. Aristotle (Q1)

Both Plato and Aristotle sought the best form of government, but their approaches diverged fundamentally. Plato's ideal state in The Republic is rationalist and hierarchical — three natural classes (Rulers, Guardians, Producers) governed by philosopher-kings whose wisdom ensures justice. Plato distrusted democracy, seeing it as a path to chaos and tyranny. Aristotle, by contrast, was more empirical. He classified six forms of government (three just, three corrupt) based on the number of rulers and their orientation toward the common good. His preferred form — Polity — combines elements of oligarchy and democracy, distributing power to the broad middle class under constitutional law. Aristotle's approach is more practical: he accepted human imperfection and sought balance rather than an ideal rationalist state. In this sense, Aristotle's Polity resembles modern constitutional democracy, while Plato's ideal state has no elected representatives and can slide into technocratic authoritarianism.

Lecture 03

Moral Foundations of Politics

Why do some people push trolleys — and others push strangers off bridges?

Comprehensive Summary

The question of whether politics should be guided by moral principles — and if so, which ones — has fascinated political philosophers for millennia. Moral Foundation Theory (Jonathan Haidt and Craig Joseph) identifies six universal cross-cultural foundations of moral judgment: Care/Harm, Fairness/Cheating, Loyalty/Betrayal, Authority/Subversion, Sanctity/Degradation, and Liberty/Oppression. Research shows that liberals tend to prioritize Care and Fairness, while conservatives invoke all six more equally. Three major political theories attempt to rationalize the moral basis of governance: Utilitarianism (maximize total happiness), Marxism (class struggle and the emancipation of the proletariat), and Social Contract Theory (individuals consent to governance in exchange for security). The Stanford Prison Experiment (Zimbardo, 1971) and Milgram's Obedience Experiment (1961) are pivotal case studies demonstrating how ordinary people can engage in harmful behavior when structures grant unchecked authority.

Haidt's Six Moral Foundations

1. Care / Harm

Empathy, compassion, protection from suffering. Central in debates about healthcare, environmental policy. Liberals prioritize this strongly. Example: Universal healthcare as a moral obligation.

2. Fairness / Cheating

Justice, reciprocity, equality. Liberals: reduce inequality. Conservatives: proportionality — rewards should match effort. Example: Debates over affirmative action and meritocracy.

3. Loyalty / Betrayal

Group identity, cohesion, partisanship. Explains strong emotional bonds to political leaders and parties. Example: Extreme reactions to "party traitors" in parliamentary systems.

4. Authority / Subversion

Respect for traditions, hierarchies, institutions. Supports belief that social order requires legitimate authority. Example: Attitudes toward military service, law enforcement.

5. Sanctity / Degradation

Purity, avoiding impure behaviors. Example: Abortion debates — "sanctity of life" vs. bodily autonomy. Also: religious values in policymaking.

6. Liberty / Oppression

Resistance to domination and unjust constraints. Left: freedom from systemic oppression (racism, economic injustice). Right: freedom from government overreach. Example: Civil rights movements; anti-taxation movements.

🚆 The Trolley Problem — Classic Utilitarian Dilemma

Scenario 1 (Switch): A runaway trolley will kill 5 people. You can pull a lever to divert it onto a side track, killing 1. Most people say: pull the lever. (Utilitarian logic: save more lives.)

Scenario 2 (Bridge): No side track. You can push a large person off a bridge to stop the trolley with their body, saving 5. Most people say: NO — this feels morally wrong even though the arithmetic is identical.

Why the difference? The trolley problem illustrates that humans don't reason purely through utilitarian calculation. Deontological intuitions (using someone merely as a means is wrong), emotional responses and moral foundations all shape judgment. This reveals the complexity of moral decision-making in politics — leaders cannot simply maximize aggregate welfare without considering individual rights and dignity.

Key Social-Political Experiments

Stanford Prison Experiment (Zimbardo, 1971)

Design: 24 male participants randomly assigned as prisoners or guards in a simulated prison at Stanford University. Intended to run 2 weeks — terminated after 6 days.

Findings: Guards rapidly began abusing power; prisoners showed extreme stress and emotional breakdown.

Political Lesson: Structure matters more than individual virtue. Authoritarian dynamics emerge when systems grant unchecked power. Political systems must institutionalize accountability and protect human dignity.

Hannah Arendt link: Connects to "banality of evil" — ordinary people can commit atrocities when systems permit and normalize it.

Milgram Obedience Experiment (1961–1963)

Context: Inspired by the Holocaust and the Eichmann trial. Stanley Milgram wanted to understand how ordinary people became complicit in genocide.

Design: Participants instructed to administer increasing "electric shocks" to a "learner" for wrong answers. The learner was an actor feigning pain. Majority continued up to the maximum voltage when an authority figure insisted.

Findings: ~65% of participants delivered the maximum shock. Obedience to authority overrode individual moral judgment.

Political Lesson: Individuals can be made complicit in harmful acts by institutional authority. Democracies must protect dissent and foster individual moral responsibility — not blind obedience.

Hannah Arendt: The "Banality of Evil"

Coined during the trial of Adolf Eichmann — a senior Nazi official who organized deportation of Jews to concentration camps. Arendt's shocking thesis: Eichmann was not a monster. He was an ordinary bureaucrat who followed orders without thinking about their moral implications. "Evil" does not require evil intent — it can emerge from the absence of moral thinking (thoughtlessness). This concept directly connects to the Authority/Subversion moral foundation: when individuals defer entirely to hierarchy, they outsource their moral agency.

Lecture 04

Political Power: Essence and Mechanisms of Realization

Who has the right to rule — and how do they keep it?

Comprehensive Summary

Power is the foundational concept of political science — it affects resource distribution, international relations, and the pursuit of interests. French and Raven (1960) identified five forms of power: coercive, reward, legitimate, referent and expert. Beyond individual interactions, political power is distinguished by its supremacy, publicity, and the state's exclusive claim to legitimate coercion. Max Weber's three types of legitimate authority — traditional, charismatic and rational-legal — explain why populations accept governance. Montesquieu's separation of powers into legislative, executive and judicial branches, combined with a system of checks and balances, is the defining institutional mechanism for preventing tyranny in modern democratic states. Kazakhstan's 2022 constitutional reforms provide a contemporary case study.

Five Forms of Power (French & Raven, 1960)

1. Coercive Power

Power through threat of punishment or negative consequences. Examples: Police arrest, military enforcement, parental punishment, economic sanctions.

2. Reward Power

Power through offering incentives. Examples: Student scholarships, tax breaks, government grants, electoral promises.

3. Legitimate Power

Power derived from formal position of authority. Examples: Elected officials, teachers, parents making decisions for children, constitutional offices.

4. Referent Power

Power derived from personal qualities — charisma, likeability, inspiration. Examples: Charismatic political leaders, celebrities, influential public figures.

5. Expert Power

Power derived from knowledge, skills and expertise. Examples: Medical doctors, economists advising governments, legal counsel, technocrats.

Lecture 05

Political Leadership and Political Elites

Who rules, why they rule, and how leaders inspire — or manipulate — the masses.

Comprehensive Summary

Every society is governed by a minority — the political elite. Classical elite theory (Pareto, Mosca, Michels) argues this is inevitable in any social system regardless of its formal structure. Pareto distinguished "foxes" (cunning) from "lions" (force) and introduced the concept of elite circulation. Mosca identified the "ruling class" as organized and intellectually superior. Michels' "iron law of oligarchy" showed that even democratic organizations eventually fall under elite control. Against this view, pluralists (Dahl, Truman, Bentley) argue that power in democracies is dispersed among competing interest groups. Leadership theories — trait, behavioral, situational, transformational — explain how individuals mobilize followers. The "cult of personality" represents the most extreme form of personalized power, associated with authoritarian regimes.

Classical Elite Theorists

Vilfredo Pareto (1848–1923)

Theory: Elites are inevitable because humans differ in ability and capacity. Two elite types: Foxes (rule through cunning, diplomacy, manipulation) and Lions (rule through force and authority). Elite circulation: history is the "graveyard of aristocracies" — elites eventually decline and are replaced by rising non-elites.

Famous Quote: "History is the graveyard of aristocracies."

Governing vs. Non-Governing Elites: Governing elites directly participate in administration; non-governing elites influence administration indirectly.

Gaetano Mosca (1858–1941)

Theory: The "ruling class" — in every society a minority (organized, skilled, resourced) dominates the majority. Elites exist in all political systems — democracies included.

Famous Quote: "In all societies, two classes of people appear — a class that rules and a class that is ruled."

Key Insight: The smaller the ruling minority relative to population, the more organized it must be. Elites maintain power through moral prestige and intellectual superiority, not just force.

Robert Michels (1876–1936)

Theory: Iron Law of Oligarchy — all organizations, even democratic ones, eventually fall under control of a small elite. As organizations grow, they require specialized leadership; centralization of power is inevitable.

Famous Quote: "Who says organization, says oligarchy."

Categories: Structural (hierarchical structures), Operational (leaders gain skills and relationships), Practical (masses lack desire for power).

Theories of Political Leadership

Trait Theory ("Great Man" Theory)

Leaders are born, not made — possessing innate characteristics: intelligence, social maturity, inner motivation, human relations skills (Kate Davis). Later refined to acknowledge that traits can be developed through experience.

Behavioral Theory

Focus on what leaders do rather than what they are. Transactional leadership: rewards and punishments for compliance. Transformational leadership: inspires followers toward a shared vision and deeper change.

Situational Theory

Leadership effectiveness depends on context. Fielder: no universally effective leader — effectiveness varies with situation. Leaders must adapt style to circumstances. "It is not meaningful to speak of an effective or ineffective leader — only a leader who is effective or ineffective in a particular situation."

Group Theory

Leaders provide benefits to followers; followers support leaders who satisfy their needs. Leadership is a reciprocal relationship of exchange and mutual dependency.

Cult of Personality

A cult of personality refers to the deliberate creation of an idealized, heroic — often godlike — public image of a political leader through propaganda, media control and symbolic gestures. It is associated with authoritarian regimes where leaders use it to consolidate power, suppress dissent and foster unquestioning loyalty.

Origin of the term: First used by Georgy Malenkov in 1953 to describe Stalin; popularized when Nikita Khrushchev used it in 1956 at the 20th Congress of the Communist Party to denounce Stalin's cult.

Historical examples: Joseph Stalin (USSR), Adolf Hitler (Germany), Mao Zedong (China), Kim dynasty (North Korea), Kim Il-sung, Saddam Hussein (Iraq).

Key features: Glorification of leader's achievements; rewriting of history; suppression of any criticism or alternative narrative; mass media control; personality iconography in public spaces.

Political danger: Cults of personality erode democratic institutions, concentrate power, destroy accountability mechanisms and create conditions for totalitarianism.

Lecture 06

Political Ideologies, Political Culture and Political Consciousness

The maps, soil and compass of political life.

Comprehensive Summary

This lecture examines three deeply interconnected concepts. Political ideologies are structured systems of ideas that interpret reality, project a vision of the future and mobilize action — from classical traditions (liberalism, conservatism, socialism, nationalism) to modern currents (environmentalism, feminism, multiculturalism). Political culture — analyzed by Almond and Verba through their landmark Civic Culture study — refers to the collective orientations (cognitive, affective, evaluative) of citizens toward political institutions. It is transmitted through socialization agents: family, education, peer groups, media and historical events. Political consciousness is the reflective awareness of one's political position, progressing from minimal (ritual participation) through reflected (critical evaluation) to transformative (collective action for change).

Four Classical Ideologies

Liberalism

Core: Individual liberty (from Latin liber = free). Natural rights, limited government, rule of law, free market, constitutionalism.

Classical vs. Modern: Classical (Locke, Smith) — minimal state, negative liberty. Modern/social (Mill, Keynes) — positive liberty; state enables individual flourishing through welfare and education.

Today: Dominant ideology of Western liberal democracies; splits into economic liberalism, welfare liberalism and neo-liberalism.

Conservatism

Core: Preserve existing institutions, values and traditions; organic society; gradual change; human imperfection requires authority and order.

Burke: Society is a living organism — tradition and custom evolved over centuries; rational re-engineering is dangerous.

Today: One-nation conservatism (social cohesion) vs. New Right (neo-liberal economics + social conservatism).

Socialism

Core: Critique of capitalism and industrial inequality; collectivism, cooperation, social equality; critique of private property.

Marx & Engels: History is class struggle; capitalism inevitably produces exploitation; proletarian revolution → socialism → communism.

Splits: Revolutionary Marxism-Leninism (USSR, China) vs. Social Democracy (reformist, welfare state, parliamentary path).

Nationalism

Core: The nation is the fundamental unit of political life; national self-determination; national identity as basis of political authority.

Forms: Liberal nationalism (self-determination), Conservative nationalism (tradition), Expansionist/chauvinist nationalism (imperialism, xenophobia).

Contradiction: Can inspire liberation movements AND fuel aggression, ethnic cleansing and war (WWI, WWII).

Modern/Post-Classical Ideologies

Environmentalism (Green Ideology)

Response to industrialization and ecological crisis. "Everything is connected to everything else" (1st law of ecology). Post-materialism: quality of life over material consumption. Radical greens: restructure industrial civilization. Reformist greens: sustainable development within existing systems. Example: Paris Climate Accord; rise of Green parties in Europe.

Feminism

Challenges patriarchy — systematic domination of women across social, economic, political and cultural spheres. Three waves: 1st (suffrage, legal rights), 2nd (workplace equality, reproductive rights), 3rd (intersectionality — race, class and gender). Key concepts: gender (social construction), bodily autonomy, equality vs. difference debate.

Multiculturalism

Recognition and respect for cultural diversity within political communities. Challenges liberal assumption of cultural neutrality. Debates: integration vs. pluralism; group rights vs. individual rights. Thinkers: Will Kymlicka, Charles Taylor ("politics of recognition").

Lecture 07

State and Civil Society

The state governs by force; civil society shapes by voice.

Comprehensive Summary

The state is the central institution of political science — defined by Max Weber as possessing a monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force within a defined territory. The state has five defining characteristics: population, territory, sovereignty, organization and legitimacy. Several theories explain its origin: patriarchal (Aristotle), divine/theological, coercive/force, social contract (Hobbes/Locke/Rousseau), hydraulic/irrigation (Wittfogel) and Marxist (class tool). States are classified by historical development (First/Second/Third world), organization (unitary/federal/confederal), head of state (monarchy/republic) and political regime (totalitarian/authoritarian/democratic). Civil society — the realm of autonomous associations between the state and the family — has grown in Kazakhstan since independence, though it faces ongoing challenges from legal restrictions and state control.

Theories of State Origin

Patriarchal Theory

Aristotle: state evolved from family → village → polis. The political community is an extension of natural human association driven by biological and social needs.

Divine/Theological Theory

Popular in medieval Europe: state established by God; rulers are divine agents. Augustine and Aquinas are key representatives. Lost popularity by late 18th century.

Coercive/Force Theory

One group conquered others through war; the strong dominated the weak. State = instrument of the powerful over the powerless.

Social Contract Theory

Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau: state is an artificial human creation; individuals voluntarily surrendered some freedoms for protection and social order.

Hydraulic Theory (Wittfogel)

In arid regions, small communities united to manage large-scale irrigation. Centralized administration of water resources → formation of the state. Classic example: Mesopotamian and Egyptian civilizations.

Marxist Theory

Marx & Engels: state arose as an instrument of the ruling class to maintain control over the means of production and suppress the working class. State = tool of class domination.

Civil Society

Civil society refers to the realm of autonomous associations, organizations and relationships that exist between the state and the family — including NGOs, pressure groups, professional associations, religious organizations, trade unions, independent media and civic movements.

Historical roots: Ancient Rome (Cicero); Enlightenment — Thomas Paine and Hegel developed the modern concept of civil society as separate from the state.

Key distinction: Civil society ≠ state. While the state governs through authority and coercion, civil society operates through voluntary association and free expression.

🏰 Civil Society in Kazakhstan

Since independence in 1991, Kazakhstan has gradually developed civil society institutions. Initial weakness stemmed from Soviet legacy of centralized governance. By the late 1990s–2000s, NGOs, independent media and community initiatives began to emerge with international support.

Government initiatives: Public councils; consultative bodies; cooperation with civil society organizations.

Challenges: Limited political freedoms; restrictive laws on assembly and association; state control over public discourse.

Active areas: Human rights, disability rights, women's rights, environmental activism, youth and children's rights.

Digital activism: Instagram, Telegram and social media have enabled new forms of civic engagement — corruption exposure, election transparency campaigns, gender-based violence awareness. Faces challenges: internet censorship, surveillance, restrictive online speech laws.

Mechanisms of Political Representation: Electoral Systems, Parties and Social Movements

How citizen voices are translated into seats, parties and power.

Comprehensive Summary

Political representation is the set of mechanisms through which citizens' interests are expressed and implemented in governance. The most fundamental mechanism is elections — formal processes through which citizens choose representatives at multiple levels of government. Electoral systems transform votes into seats through four main types: plurality (first-past-the-post), majority/runoff, proportional representation and mixed systems. Each system has distinct consequences for party formation, minority representation and government stability. Political parties organize around shared ideology to win elections and govern. Typologies range from elite/cadre parties to mass parties to catch-all parties. Party systems vary from no-party to single, dominant, two-party and multi-party configurations. Social movements organize outside formal political institutions to influence public opinion and policy from the bottom up.

Four Types of Electoral Systems

SystemHow It WorksStrengthsWeaknessesExamples
Plurality (FPTP) Candidate with most votes wins, even without majority Simple, fast, clear mandates, strong government Disadvantages small parties; wasted votes; low proportionality UK, USA, Canada, India
Majority (Two-Round) Requires >50% to win; second round if no majority Winners have broader support; prevents narrow-majority government Costly; time-consuming; still limits small parties France (presidential); many Latin American countries
Proportional Representation (PR) Seats allocated proportionally to vote share; party lists Diverse representation; minority parties gain voice; high voter satisfaction May produce fragmented parliaments; coalition instability Germany, Netherlands, Israel, most EU states
Mixed (Combined) Combines plurality/majority single-member districts with PR party lists Balances local representation and overall proportionality More complex; risk of two-tier inequality Germany (MMP), Kazakhstan, Japan, New Zealand

Key Electoral Concepts

Electoral Formula

The rule for converting votes into seats. Majoritarian/plurality: most votes wins. Proportional: seats match vote proportions.

District Magnitude

Number of representatives elected per district. Higher magnitude (more seats per district) → more proportional outcomes; easier for small parties to win seats.

Electoral Threshold

Minimum vote percentage for a party to enter parliament. Kazakhstan: 5% (Majilis) and 7% (local Maslikhats). Germany: 5%. Purpose: prevents extreme fragmentation.

Ballot Structure

How voters make their choices: single candidate, party list, ranked choice. Affects voter agency and party strategies.

Duverger's Law

Plurality/FPTP elections → two-party systems. Proportional representation → multi-party systems. Political scientist Maurice Duverger's foundational finding.

First-Order vs. Second-Order Elections

First-order: national elections with high stakes (leadership change). Second-order: local, midterm elections with lower turnout and attention.

Lecture 09

National Security in the Age of Digitalization: Information Wars, Challenges of the Era of Deepfakes and Fake News

The battlefield is now the information space — and every citizen is on the front line.

Comprehensive Summary

The 21st century has fundamentally transformed the nature of national security. Traditional security — defined by territorial defense, military deterrence and sovereign borders — has been supplemented and in some ways superseded by threats in the information domain. Digitalization, globalization and AI-driven technologies have created an environment where cyber attacks can paralyze economies, disinformation can undermine elections, and deepfakes can create strategic confusion without a single bullet being fired. Information itself has become weaponized. Key phenomena include: information warfare (systematic manipulation of perceptions through narratives and algorithms), disinformation (deliberate falsehoods), misinformation (unintentional errors), propaganda (ideological manipulation), fake news (fabricated journalism), and deepfakes (AI-generated hyper-realistic media manipulation). Kazakhstan, integrated into global information networks and located at the crossroads of competing geopolitical narratives, faces specific vulnerabilities illustrated by the January 2022 events and the COVID-19 infodemic.

Core Concepts of Digital Security

Information Warfare

The use of narratives, images, algorithms and emotions — rather than weapons — to undermine an adversary's will, disorient populations and weaken institutions. Less visible than military force but potentially more damaging to democratic governance and social trust.

Disinformation

Deliberate spread of false or misleading information to manipulate perceptions. Key distinction: intent to deceive. Examples: Russian interference in 2016 US election; Brexit campaign false statistics; COVID-19 vaccine conspiracy theories.

Misinformation

False information spread without deliberate intent to deceive — often through ignorance, misunderstanding or careless sharing. Still dangerous — spreads quickly through social networks.

Propaganda

The systematic promotion of particular ideological or political narratives by a government or organization, typically employing emotional appeal, selective framing and repetition. Not always false — but always strategically selective.

Fake News

Fabricated stories presented as legitimate journalism, designed to mislead, manipulate or provoke. Amplified by social media algorithms that prioritize emotionally engaging content. MIT research: false news spreads 70% more than true stories and reaches 1,500 people up to 6 times faster.

Deepfakes

AI-generated hyper-realistic video/audio recordings showing individuals saying or doing things they never did. Exploit evolutionary human trust in sensory experience. Can be used for political manipulation, economic fraud and military psychological operations.

Hybrid Warfare

Blending of conventional military operations with cyber attacks, disinformation campaigns, economic pressure and proxy forces. Visible in Russia-Ukraine conflict: digital propaganda, cyber operations and military action worked in tandem.

Infodemic

Term coined by WHO during COVID-19: a flood of information — both accurate and inaccurate — that makes it difficult for citizens to find reliable health guidance. WHO-studied: 85% of sample tweets about COVID-19 contained false claims.

Cyber Warfare

State-sponsored or non-state attacks on digital infrastructure — government databases, power grids, banking systems, electoral systems, hospitals. Can cause physical damage and economic disruption without military invasion.

Digital Authoritarianism

Use of digital technologies by authoritarian governments to monitor, control and suppress citizens and political opposition — including internet censorship, mass surveillance, social credit systems and targeted harassment campaigns.

OSINT (Open-Source Intelligence)

Intelligence gathered from publicly available sources — social media, satellite images, news reports. Used by journalists (Bellingcat), governments and citizens to verify or challenge disinformation in real-time. Increasingly important in conflict situations.

Post-Truth Politics

A political culture in which emotion and personal belief consistently outweigh objective fact in public discourse. Hannah Arendt: "The greatest danger of constant lying is not that people believe lies, but that they eventually stop believing in anything at all."

World Policy and Modern International Relations

In a world without a global government, how do states cooperate, compete and survive?

Comprehensive Summary

International relations analyzes how states and non-state actors interact in a global system that lacks a single central authority. Three major theoretical traditions dominate the field: Realism (states pursue survival in an anarchic system through power and deterrence), Liberalism (cooperation is possible through institutions, trade and international law), and Constructivism (the world is socially constructed through ideas, beliefs, identity and norms — not just material power). Three transformative historical forces have reshaped global politics: globalization (economic, cultural and information interdependence), westernization (adoption of western values and institutions by non-western societies) and modernization (transformation from traditional to modern political, economic and social systems). The global policy landscape features multiple actors: nation-states (most powerful), intergovernmental organizations (UN, WTO, NATO, WHO, UNESCO), multinational corporations, NGOs, epistemic communities and norm entrepreneurs. The balance of power operates in three configurations: unipolar (post-Cold War US dominance), bipolar (Cold War USA-USSR) and multipolar (emerging today with China, Russia, EU and others as competing poles). Kazakhstan pursues a multi-vector foreign policy, balancing relations with Russia, China, the EU and the US.

Realism: Five Core Propositions

  1. Governments cannot count on peaceful, cooperative human nature to produce harmonious interactions — conflict is the natural state of affairs.
  2. Geographically-based sovereign states are the central actors in the international system.
  3. State behavior is rational — guided by national interest, defined primarily in terms of survival, security, power and relative capabilities.
  4. There is no "world police" — the international system is anarchic; each state must rely primarily on itself (self-help).
  5. Foreign policy must be based on a state's need to protect and advance its own power — not on morality, if power and morality come into conflict.

Classic example: Cold War arms race — both superpowers built up arsenals not out of desire for conflict but because in an anarchic system, each had to assume the worst about the other's intentions (security dilemma).

Liberalism: Core Ideas

  1. Human nature is capable of peaceful cooperation when the right institutional conditions are created.
  2. Democratic Peace Theory: Democracies rarely go to war with each other — shared values and domestic accountability constrain leaders.
  3. Economic Interdependence: When countries are economically connected through trade, investment and supply chains, war becomes irrational — it would harm both sides economically.
  4. States should follow moral principles in foreign policy; human rights and humanitarian norms are legitimate global concerns.
  5. International Organizations (UN, WTO, EU, NATO) can enhance cooperation, reduce transaction costs and make agreements credible.

Key example: EU integration — France and Germany, once bitter enemies in three wars, now anchor a peaceful democratic union through economic interdependence and shared institutions.

Constructivism: Key Insights

Constructivism argues that the social world — including international relations — is not simply "given" but is constructed through human interaction, shared beliefs and intersubjective meaning.

  • Alexander Wendt's famous phrase: "Anarchy is what states make of it" — the same anarchic structure produces very different behaviors depending on how states perceive each other.
  • Two countries with equal military power but who see each other as friends (USA-Canada) behave entirely differently from two countries who see each other as enemies (USA-North Korea).
  • Norm entrepreneurs like Malala Yusafzai or Greta Thunberg can reshape international norms and pressure states to act — even without formal power.
  • State identity (democratic, Islamic, revolutionary, etc.) shapes foreign policy preferences beyond simple security calculations.

Model Answer: Realism vs. Liberalism vs. Constructivism (Q1)

The three dominant paradigms of international relations theory each offer valuable but partial explanations of world politics. Realism views states as rational self-interested actors in an anarchic international system without a world government. Because there is no higher authority to guarantee security, states must rely on self-help, building military power and forming alliances to survive. This explains the Cold War arms race, China's naval build-up in the South China Sea and Russia's assertion of sphere-of-influence claims in post-Soviet space. However, realism struggles to explain why democracies rarely fight each other, why states cooperate voluntarily through institutions like the WTO, or why the EU — comprising former enemies — has maintained 75+ years of peace. Liberalism addresses these gaps by emphasizing that democratic peace, economic interdependence and international institutions create incentives for cooperation. The EU, Paris Climate Agreement and UN peacekeeping missions are liberal successes. Yet liberalism underestimates the persistence of great-power competition, as evident in Russia-Ukraine and US-China rivalry. Constructivism adds a dimension both theories miss: the role of ideas, identity and norms. Alexander Wendt argues that "anarchy is what states make of it" — the same international structure produces entirely different behavior depending on how states perceive each other. Two nuclear-armed states who see each other as friends (USA-UK) behave differently from two who see each other as enemies (USA-North Korea). No single theory is complete: contemporary world politics is best understood by combining realist insights about power with liberal appreciation of institutions and constructivist recognition of the role of norms and identity.
